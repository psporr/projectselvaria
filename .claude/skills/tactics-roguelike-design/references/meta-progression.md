# Meta-Progression

Meta-progression is anything that survives a run ending in death and makes the *next* run different. It's the answer to "if I just lost, why should I immediately go again?" — and how much of it you have is one of the most consequential dials in the genre, because it trades off against the game staying equally skill-tested over its whole lifetime.

## The spectrum, anchored by two shipped answers

**Slay the Spire: minimal meta-progression.** Almost nothing about a run's actual difficulty changes based on how many runs you've played. Unlocks are mostly *new cards added to the pool* (more variety, not more power) and cosmetic/card-back rewards; Ascension levels are a difficulty *selector* the player opts into, not something that ratchets automatically. The result: a run played by a 500-hour veteran and a run played by someone on their 5th attempt are mechanically the same fight — only the player's knowledge differs. This keeps the game a permanent, legitimate challenge and makes every win feel earned rather than bought, at the cost of a loss "buying" the player very little beyond card-pool familiarity and account-level unlocks.

**Hades: heavy, deliberate meta-progression.** The Mirror of Night (permanent passive upgrades bought with Darkness), weapon Aspects (alternate forms of each weapon, unlocked and then upgraded with Titan Blood), and various other currencies (Nectar for relationship/keepsake unlocks, Ambrosia, gemstones for the hub) all persist across deaths and make the player *mechanically* stronger over time, independent of skill. This is intentional and load-bearing for the game's design: Hades is also a narrative game where the story is told incrementally across many runs, so death is reframed as "returning home to advance the story," not "failure with no payoff" — meta-progression is the mechanic that makes that framing feel earned rather than hollow. Once meta-progression is mostly maxed, **Heat** (the Pact of Punishment) becomes the difficulty lever instead — a menu of opt-in modifiers (tougher enemies, fewer resources, an extra objective) each contributing to a Heat score, with better rewards at higher Heat. This hands difficulty-scaling control to the player explicitly, rather than the game guessing what a veteran needs.

**Darkest Dungeon: meta-progression as attrition management, not power growth.** The hamlet's upgrades (stagecoach, guild, blacksmith, etc.) raise the *ceiling* of what your roster can become, but the core tension — heroes accumulate stress and can be permanently lost — isn't something meta-progression erases. Meta-progression here mostly widens your options (more recruits, more upgrade choices) rather than flatly raising power, which keeps the core risk (losing a hero you've invested in) meaningful throughout.

## The two failure modes to avoid

**Too little**: a run that ends in death and returns the player to a *literally identical* starting state, over and over, with the only difference being their own memorized knowledge, works for a genre built entirely around "get better at the game" (Spire, most roguelikes proper) — but if your game is also trying to sell a sense of *character* growth or long-term investment (naming units, building a roster over time — which Fire-Emblem-adjacent tactics games usually want), zero meta-progression can read as punishing rather than pure and fair, because the player's attachment to their specific squad is real even if the game doesn't reward it structurally.

**Too much**: if a returning player's meta-progressed power makes early encounters trivial, two things break at once — first-time players and veterans no longer have a comparable experience (bad for anyone watching streams/guides, bad for difficulty tuning since there's no single "the game" to balance around), and the run itself starts to feel like a formality gating the *real* game (grinding meta-currency), rather than being the game.

## A middle path: progression that's real but doesn't trivialize

Consider separating meta-progression into categories with different trust levels:
- **Unlocks that add variety, not power** (new options in the draft pool, new starting choices) — Spire's approach, safest against power creep.
- **Progression that widens the roster/options but resets per-run power** (new recruitable units become available to draft into a run, but their in-run stats still start at a run-appropriate baseline) — keeps a run's moment-to-moment challenge stable while still rewarding long-term investment.
- **A player-set difficulty dial** (Heat/Ascension-style), especially once other meta-progression exists — this is close to mandatory once you have real permanent power growth, because it's the only way to keep the game challenging for both a first-run player and a hundred-runs-in veteran without maintaining two different games.
- **Permanent stat/power growth with a hard ceiling** — if you do want raw power to carry over, cap it low enough that a fully-progressed player is meaningfully stronger but not trivializing, and playtest specifically at "max meta-progression, first real attempt at the hardest content" to find where the ceiling should sit.

## Failing forward

Whatever you choose, make sure a *lost* run still banks something toward the next attempt — even a small, mostly-cosmetic currency reframes death as "a return trip with progress," not "wasted time." This is less about the exact numbers and more about the emotional framing: Hades' whole narrative structure exists to make failure feel like forward motion; even without a story to lean on, a small guaranteed reward for a failed run (a currency tick, a fragment of an unlock) does real work toward the same feeling.
